Japanese & Arabic, side by side

I am learning Japanese from zero — I could not read a single character in July 2026. Alongside it I am filling a gap I have carried for years: I read and pronounce Arabic fluently but never understood it. Every Japanese sentence I learn gets translated into Arabic too, so one set of sentences teaches two languages.

One lesson per day, built from my teacher's lecture notes · がんばります · بِالتَّوْفِيقِ

2.Grammar lessons

The teacher's own grammar sheets, in their own numbered track. A day lesson brings new words; a grammar lesson brings none — it names the rules those words have been running on all along. Each rule is taught the way the sheet states it, mirrored in Arabic, with audio on every example sentence.

#Grammar lessonCoversTime
1 Six rules, one sentence The six grammar points of 第1課, each an operation on わたしは がくせいです: the 〜は 〜です frame and why は reads wa; the four shapes of です against Arabic's لَيْسَ and كَانَ; か at the end of a question against هَلْ at the start; も replacing は where Arabic adds أَيْضًا; の as the mirror image of the iḍāfa; and 〜さん, which is given and never claimed 16 min

3.Reading chapters

A different kind of page, kept out of the numbered lessons on purpose. A lesson is built from a day's material — reordered, cut, explained. A reading chapter is a printed page reproduced: the textbook's own lines, in its order, with its own furigana. Every line has a button that reads it aloud and a button that opens the English and the Arabic, and both start closed.

#ChapterCoversTime
1 Minna no Nihongo I, lessons 1–3 Ninety-three lines — the 文型 patterns, 例文 examples and the three 会話 dialogues of the book's first three lessons: introducing yourself, saying whose things are whose, and asking where something is and what it costs. Every line in Japanese, English and Arabic with audio in both spoken languages; the notes flag the five lines whose furigana does not match how the line is actually said 25 min

4.Practice

Routines, not lessons: nothing on them is new. They exist to keep what the lessons taught from slipping away, and they are the only pages here that remember anything about how you did — in your own browser, never sent anywhere.

#RoutineCoversTime
1 Flash Cards All 320 words of lessons 10 to 17 in one deck, plus the 20 counter readings that change — flip-cards asked from whichever side comes up, thirty a session, dealt fresh every time you load the page. It weights the words you keep missing, and builds a trouble-words session out of them 15 min
2 The Reading Plan Ten minutes of reading fluency a day, all steered from one page: re-read the course's own Reading 1 until it runs at the audio's speed, then climb Tadoku's 76 free graded readers level by level — every book linked, most with read-aloud audio, each ticked off here as you finish it. 5.Reference sheets

The compressed version of what each lesson taught — made to print and keep beside the paper.

  • Hiragana stroke chart — all 46 characters with every stroke numbered in writing order, the shapes that get confused with each other, and every example word glossed in English and Arabic.
  • Katakana stroke chart — all 71 katakana (46 basic, 25 marked) with every stroke numbered, each one beside the hiragana that shares its sound, which pairs come from the same ancient kanji and which do not, and the shapes that get mistaken for each other — ranked by a writing study of 430 Japanese children rather than by the usual lists.
  • Tenten and maru — the 25 marked characters, each beside the one it is built from, with stroke order and the three that do not follow the rule; plus the 73 words the marks unlock.
  • Yōon — the small ゃ ゅ ょ — all 33 contracted sounds in both scripts with a common word for each, the three rows whose romaji drops its y, the pairs where size decides the word, and the 45 words the small kana unlock. The two charts have different holes, and between them only one cell is empty in both.
  • Two-kana word list — eighty words that are exactly two characters long and use nothing but the 46 basic hiragana, in nine meaning groups, with the Arabic for each.
  • Question words — the thirty-two interrogatives in four stems, the こそあど grid six of them come from, the point-against-stretch grid that かん builds, the なに/なん rule, and eleven ready-made questions in all three languages.
  • The calendar — the seven days beside the planets they are named after, all twelve months in Japanese and in both Arabic systems, every date from the 1st to the 31st with the four that never regularised, and the twelve old lunisolar month names.
  • One day, every word — all 230 words taught so far, walked through as a single day in fourteen scenes, each word appearing exactly once. The question words are the joints between scenes, which is what makes them stick.
  • Counters and their sound changes — all eight counters taught so far (months, days, age, floors, yen, hours, minutes, people) from 1 to 10, every euphonic change flagged and tappable to hear. Led by the question that predicts the whole table: does the counter start on a vowel, a voiced sound, or a voiceless one? — because only the voiceless ones ever change. Read from the other side, four numbers never change anything at all.
  • Classroom phrasebook — the 16 phrases in Japanese and Modern Standard Arabic, with audio, and the four grammar patterns that generate most of them.

6.How this works

My teacher hands out lecture notes. Each day's notes become one lesson here — not a copy of them, but the same material with the things a sheet of paper cannot do: stroke-order animation you can replay, audio in both languages, and — where a thing is only learned by being asked — a drill that asks.

The Arabic is Modern Standard Arabic with full harakat. That is deliberate: decoding the script has never been my problem, so the vowel marks cost me nothing and remove every ambiguity about meaning — which is the part I am actually here for.

A note on the audio

Every 🔊 button uses your own browser's speech voices — nothing is downloaded and no server is involved. If a button looks faded, your device has no voice installed for that language. On macOS you can add one under System Settings → Accessibility → Spoken Content → System Voice → Manage Voices. Synthesised speech is close but not perfect; for Japanese pitch accent, trust NHK's recordings over it.
