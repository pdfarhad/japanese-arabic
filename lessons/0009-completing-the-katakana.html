Lesson 9 · カタカナ — finishing the chart

The last twenty-one, and the kanji still showing through

Twenty-one characters finish the second script — and then the marks, the small kana and the long-vowel bar finish katakana itself. Several of the new shapes barely changed from the kanji they were cut out of — ハ is the whole of 八, ミ is 三 tilted — and that, rather than their simplicity, is what makes them easy to get wrong.

Lesson 9 of the course

Twenty-five plus twenty-one is forty-six, and forty-six is the whole of katakana — the same arithmetic that finished the hiragana in lesson 2. Every sound below is one you have been saying since the first week. What is new is only which marks carry it.

1.What is left of the grid

Four consonant rows, one row of two, and one character that belongs to no row at all. The holes are in exactly the places they were in hiragana, and for exactly the same reasons.

Two things on that table are not quite what they look like. The w row's last cell, ヲ, is a character modern Japanese does not write at all — section 6 is about why it is still on the chart. And ヘ in the h row is not a printing accident: it really is the same shape as its hiragana.

2.The kanji still showing through

Lesson 8 set out the operation that made these shapes: hiragana is a whole kanji written in flowing cursive, katakana is one piece of a kanji kept straight. In this half of the chart that piece is often almost the entire character.

Trick 1 · The visible ancestor is a trap here, not a help

Because so little was cut away, several of these characters are still nearly their source kanji — and that is precisely where Japanese children go wrong. The writing study behind section 4 marks a character down whenever it could be mistaken for its lookalike kanji, and names four of them explicitly: セ/七, モ/毛, チ/千 and ミ/三.1 Two of those are the character and its own ancestor. Knowing where a shape came from tells you why it looks like that; it does not tell you which of the two you are looking at.

Fourteen are siblings. Five are strangers. Two are arguments.

Same question as day 8, asked of the remaining pairs: did the katakana and its hiragana come from the same kanji? Where they did, the family likeness is inherited and can be leaned on. Where they did not, any resemblance is an accident of two independent simplifications.

The two arguments are マ and ン, and they are unsettled in different ways. For マ the sources split between 末 and 万; since ま is certainly 末, whether that pair are siblings is itself the dispute — the same position ニ was in on day 8. ン is unsettled far more deeply: proposals include 尓, 无 and 二, and the strongest may not be a kanji at all but the old annotation mark for a nasal beat.3 Its hiragana is not in doubt — ん is 无 written cursively.

Trick 2 · Katakana are still not the shorter script

Day 8's twenty-five took 62 strokes against hiragana's 57. These twenty-one come out at 46 against 47 — a dead heat. Add both halves and the whole of katakana is 108 strokes against hiragana's 104. The angular script is not the quicker one; its advantage is that every stroke is a straight line or a simple hook, so it is easier to get right.

Four cards carry a warning rather than a word list

ム, ヤ and ル almost never begin a word, so their cards show words that contain them instead — the same arrangement ヌ needed on day 8. ン never begins one at all. And ヲ has no word list because there are no words; see section 6.

4.The ones that actually get written wrong

Which katakana cause trouble is a question with a measured answer rather than an opinion. A 2019 study tested all 45 katakana in handwriting on 430 Japanese children in school years 1–3 and sorted every error into five kinds.1 The findings do not match the usual beginners' list, and the mismatch is the useful part.

Trick 3 · ソ and ン are the same puzzle as シ and ツ, with one mark instead of two

Day 8 promised this rule would settle when ン arrived. It does, unchanged — read the direction the pen travels, not the finished picture:

  • ン — n: the short mark comes in from the left, and the long stroke is pulled upward from the bottom left. Same family as シ.
  • ソ — so: the short mark comes down from the top, and the long stroke is pulled downward to the left. Same family as ツ.

The giveaway on a finished letter is where the strokes begin: ン's start down the left-hand side, ソ's start along the top. This substitution — writing ン for ソ — was the single commonest one in the study, and it was still happening in year three.

Trick 4 · For ヨ, ユ and コ, check which side the horizontals attach to

ヨ is the most mirror-written character in the whole script — 18 of 430 children drew it backwards. Its three horizontals all meet the upright on the right, making a comb whose teeth point left. ミ is second, for a related reason: all three of its strokes sweep right to left, and reversing that habit reverses the character. Neither is a memory problem — write them slowly once, in the right direction, and watch the start of each stroke.

Trick 5 · Five of these fail on a blank page, not on a lookalike

ハ, ワ, ロ, ユ and ヨ lead the "left it blank" column: the children were not confusing them with anything, they simply could not produce them. That needs a different exercise from the drill below. Recognition — seeing ワ and knowing it says wa — will not fix it. Write them from the sound, on paper, with the chart face down.

One pair worth demoting, on the same evidence that demoted ク/タ on day 8: ワ against ク is in every beginner's list and appears nowhere in this study's error data. What Japanese children actually write instead of ワ is ウ. Both are worth a glance; only one is attested.

5.Read them cold

A character appears, you name its sound — four options, by tapping or by pressing 1–4. There is deliberately no play button on the question, because hearing it is the answer. Miss one and it stops, names the hiragana that shares the sound, shows how the strokes go, and lists the words from the cards above that contain it.

The wrong answers come from the table in section 4 before anywhere else — ル against レ, リ against ソ, マ against ユ, ヨ against ヲ. Only then does it fall back to the same row, and only then to anywhere. Wrong answers that are obviously wrong measure nothing.

6.ヲ, the character that retired

ヲ is on the chart, has a stroke order, and partners を — which means, as lesson 2 established, that it is pronounced simply o. It is also a character you can read a great deal of modern Japanese without ever meeting. Three independent signs of that:

  1. The object particle is always written を, in hiragana, even in a sentence otherwise thick with katakana. That was を's only job — so it took the whole job with it.
  2. Foreign words with a wo sound are now spelled ウォ, not ヲ.4
  3. The dictionary has no entry for a single word containing it, and the writing study in section 4 tested 45 characters, not 46 — it left ヲ out on the grounds that children are not taught to write it.1

So learn it to read — it turns up in documents written before the 1946 reforms, when ordinary Japanese was set in kanji and katakana rather than kanji and hiragana — and do not go looking for a use for it. This is the same kind of honest gap as day 5's ぱ row and day 7's ぴ row: the absence is the fact, not a hole in the chart.

7.ー, the mark that holds a vowel

Day 8 ended by naming the one thing its words needed and its chart did not contain. Here it is. ー — the chōonpu — is not a letter and not a dash. It repeats whatever vowel came before it for one more beat.

This is why so many of the words on the cards above carry it: ヒーター, メール, ラーメン, ヨーロッパ. Leave it out and the word is not merely mispronounced, it is misspelled.

Trick 6 · One mark, whichever vowel — and that is the difference from Arabic

Arabic writes a long vowel by writing a letter, and which letter depends on the vowel: ا lengthens a in بَاب, و lengthens u, ي lengthens i. Katakana uses one neutral mark for all five — ー simply inherits the vowel to its left. So you never choose which lengthener to write; you only decide whether the beat is there. Hiragana, confusingly, does it the Arabic way instead, by writing the vowel out: おかあさん, not おかーさん.

One practical note: in vertically-set Japanese the mark turns with the text and is written as a vertical stroke, not a horizontal one. It is the only character on the chart that changes direction with the writing.

Trick 7 · The パ row was the hard one in hiragana. Here it is the easiest

Lesson 5 had to bend its own rule for this row: there is no common two-kana word in the language containing ぱ ぴ ぷ ぺ ぽ, because in native Japanese a p only turns up after ん or っ. In katakana the same five characters are among the most useful on the chart — パン, ピアノ, プール, ペン, ポスト.

The reversal is not a coincidence, it is the same fact twice. A word can start with p in Japanese essentially only if it was borrowed — and katakana is the script borrowed words are written in. The row hiragana could barely fill is the row katakana lives in.

Trick 8 · ヂ and ヅ are dead here too

ヂ sounds exactly like ジ and ヅ exactly like ズ, and modern katakana simply does not use them — the dictionary has no word containing either, so both cards below are empty on purpose. This is the same absence ぢ and づ had on day 5, and the same kind of absence as ヲ in section 6. Learn them to recognise; never reach for them when writing. When you hear ji or zu, it is ジ or ズ.

The arithmetic of a mark

A tenten always adds exactly two strokes to its base and a maru always exactly one — true of all 25 here, as it was of all 25 in hiragana. So these 25 katakana take 103 strokes against their hiragana's 104: another dead heat, and the same answer as Trick 2. Each card below splits the count for you — the strokes of the base, then the mark.

One character exists in katakana that hiragana has no version of at all: ヴ, which is ウ wearing the tenten and gives a v sound for foreign words. It is not on the chart above because it is not one of the standard forty-six-plus-twenty-five, and in practice you will meet the same word spelled both ways — ヴァイオリン and バイオリン are both current, and the dictionary tags both common. Read it; you do not need to write it.

Tell the marked from the unmarked

A second drill, over the four base rows and their marked forms together — カ サ タ ハ against ガ ザ ダ バ パ. This is the only thing in the section that is genuinely new to practise: the shapes are known and the rule is known, so what is left is noticing a diacritic the eye is trained to skip.

9.The small ャ ュ ョ, and the holes they fill

Same rule as lesson 7, and the same arithmetic. Eleven characters end in -i and can be palatalised — キ シ チ ニ ヒ ミ リ plus ギ ジ ビ ピ — times three small vowels, is 33. The small kana replaces the i, it does not follow it: キ + ャ is kya, one beat, not ki-ya.

Between them they use them all — except one.

Trick 10 · ピャ is the last empty cell in the language

Of the 33, exactly one has no common word in either script: ぴゃ/ピャ. This is the fourth time the p row has been the awkward one — day 5 had to bend its word rule for it, day 7 dashed out all three of its cells, and section 8 above showed the full-size パ row reversing into the easiest row on the chart. All four are the same underlying fact: a Japanese p is either borrowed, or it follows ン or ッ. Nobody has borrowed a pya.

10.The small ッ — a beat of silence

The last small character, and the only one still outstanding in both scripts. ッ is a small ツ and makes no tsu sound at all. It doubles the consonant that follows it — and it takes a full beat while doing nothing audible.

That makes three things in katakana that take a beat without being a syllable: ン, the long-vowel ー from section 7, and now ッ. Japanese counts beats, not letters — this is the same point lesson 2 made about ん, arriving for the third time.

Trick 11 · You already know this from شَدَّة

Arabic doubles a consonant with the shadda — مُدَرِّس (mudarris), where the mark sits on the doubled letter itself. Japanese doubles a consonant by putting a whole separate character in front of it. Same idea, opposite method — and it is exactly the split lesson 2 found with を against the accusative ending: Japanese hangs a character off the word, Arabic changes the letter.

The useful part for reading: if you can already hear the difference between دَرَسَ and دَرَّسَ, you can already hear the difference between カプ and カップ. It is not a new sound, only a new spelling of one you have.

Two rules worth having: ッ never begins a word, and it never sits before a vowel — there has to be a consonant for it to double. It is everywhere in the vocabulary already on this page:

11.The sounds katakana had to invent

Hiragana has five small vowels too, and essentially never uses them. Katakana uses them constantly, because katakana has a job hiragana does not: spelling foreign words that contain sounds Japanese does not have. There is no fa, no ti, no je in native Japanese — so katakana builds them.
