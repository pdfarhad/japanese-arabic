Lesson 6 · Interrogatives

Four stems, thirty-two questions

Thirty-four lines on the day's sheet, and thirty-two of them are questions. They are not thirty-two things to learn: every one begins with one of four sounds, and almost every one is a shorter word you already have with something ordinary stuck on the end. The remaining two lines are not questions at all — they are the answers that arrived alongside them.

≈17 minutes · every reading checked against Jisho.org · the こそあど sets after Tofugu

Lesson 6 of the course

Five lessons of characters and words, and nothing yet that asks for anything back. This one is entirely question words — and it is the first lesson where the right move is not to memorise the list.

1.The first sound tells you it is a question

Trick 1 · You know it is a question before the word ends

A Japanese sentence puts its verb last, so you often wait a long time to find out what is happening. Question words are the exception: the very first mora announces itself. Hear ど and something is about to be picked out of a set; hear なん and the field is wide open; hear い and a number is wanted. You can start assembling an answer before the speaker has finished asking.

Arabic gives you no such warning. مَنْ, مَا, مَتَى, أَيْنَ, كَيْفَ, كَمْ and أَيّ share no opening letter and no pattern — each one has to be known on its own. Japanese charges you two syllables and hands back the whole set.

だれ and どこ are not new: both were word cards in lesson 5, filed under people and under town. Today they go to work.

2.Six of them come with three free relatives

Six of the twelve ど-words are not standalone vocabulary at all. Each is the last member of a set of four called こそあど — the name is just the four opening kana read in order. The set works like this:

  • こ — near me
  • そ — near you
  • あ — away from both of us
  • ど — which?

The rest of the word — the ending — says what kind of thing is being pointed at. Prefix and ending are fully independent, which is what makes the grid below a grid rather than a list. Tap any cell to hear it.

The grid needs JavaScript. The same seven sets are laid out on the question-word reference sheet.

Trick 2 · Learn a row, not a word

Read the grid across and you learn an ending; read it down and you learn a distance. Twenty-eight words, seven endings and four prefixes — so the honest amount of new material here is eleven items, not twenty-eight. When you meet an unknown こ-word later, chop off the first mora and look for the row.

Two members refuse to be predicted, and both are marked in the grid: あそこ is not あこ, and ああ is not あう. Both irregulars are in the あ column, and neither is a ど-word, so nothing you need today is affected.

Why the polite one is the long one

Two of today's words are marked polite, and both are the longer member of a pair: だれ → どなた, and どこ → どちら. That is not a coincidence to be memorised twice. It is one rule that runs through the whole language.

Trick 3 · Politeness is distance, and distance costs syllables

The polite form asks something slightly beside the point. どちら asks a direction rather than a place; どなた comes from the row that gave modern Japanese あなた, “you”. Refusing to point straight at the thing is what makes it courteous, and the extra mora is what you pay for it. Expect this everywhere: when two Japanese words mean the same thing, the longer one is the politer one.

Arabic does the same and you already do it without thinking — مَنْ حَضْرَتُكَ؟ is longer than مَنْ أَنْتَ؟ for exactly this reason.

There is an eighth set the grid leaves out: こっち · そっち · あっち · どっち, a casual contraction of the ちら row and very common in speech. It needs the small っ, which this course has not taught yet, so it is named here and drilled later.

The tail tells you what has to come next

That is the use of knowing the seam, and it is not a curiosity. Knowing a word means knowing where it is allowed to sit, and for all seven of these the tail is what says so:

  • の demands a noun after it. どこの and なんの can never end a sentence — どこの くるま, なんの ほん. Japanese A の B is English “B of A”, so the question word comes first and the thing you are asking about follows. The same の also gives you だれの, “whose”, which is not even on your list.
  • ぐらい asks for an amount, and an amount can be the answer on its own — so どのぐらいですか is a finished sentence.
  • いう behaves like の: a noun must follow (どういう いみ).
  • で names the means — the thing something is done with or by. That is why なんで literally asks “by what”, and why it can be closed with です on its own: なんでですか is a finished sentence.
  • と, して and やって point at an action, so they want a verb. That is exactly why どうやって and だれと are the two on this page you cannot use fully yet — verbs arrive in a later lesson. Until then, borrow one whole, as よみますか is borrowed above, or leave the verb out and say だれとですか.

Trick 4 · Never store a compound; store the seam

Learn eight words and you have eight words. Learn five tails — の, と, で, ぐらい, and the て-form that して and やって are — and you get these eight today plus an open-ended set later. の joins any two nouns, と turns any noun into “with that”, で turns any noun into “by means of that”, and ぐらい makes any quantity approximate.

The て-form is the best of the four and you have just met it without being told: して is it for する (“to do”), やって for やる (also “to do”, more hands-on). It is the shape that lets a Japanese verb attach to anything else, and you will use it every day for years.

Three ways to say why

The day's sheet hands you three words for “why”, which looks like generosity and is actually a decision you now have to make every time. They are not interchangeable, and the difference is register far more than meaning:

WordWhere it belongsWhat else it can mean
なぜ
naze		 Writing, and speech that is being careful. The formal one. Nothing. It is the only one of the three that can never be misread.
どうして
dōshite		 Anywhere. The safe middle, and the one to reach for by default. Also “in what manner” — and native speakers sometimes have to ask which was meant.
なんで
nan de		 Friends, family, anyone you are relaxed with. Blunt in the wrong room. Literally “by what means”, which is where the “why” came from in the first place.

Set against those, どうやって is the easy one: it can only mean “by what method”. It cannot be misread at all, so use it freely. That is the asymmetry worth holding on to — どうやって is narrow and safe; どうして is broad and occasionally ambiguous.

Arabic keeps all of this apart without effort, which makes it a useful place to stand: لِمَاذَا for the plain reason, لِأَيِّ سَبَبٍ for the formal one, لِمَ for the short and casual one, and بِأَيِّ طَرِيقَةٍ for the method. Four Arabic phrases, four Japanese words, and they line up.

Trick 5 · いつ and なんじ are the same two characters

Both are written 何時. Read them nan-ji and you have pinned the question to a clock face — “what hour”. Read them itsu and the unit is left open: the answer could be half past four, could be next March.

So the choice is not about politeness or formality, it is about how big the answer is allowed to be. If you do not know what unit it will come back in, ask いつ. If you want a time on a clock, ask なんじ.

The other two share a different piece: いく (幾), an old word for “how many”. Put つ after it — the all-purpose counter for things — and you have いくつ. Put ら after it and you have いくら, which counts money and nothing else. Two words, one stem, and the tail decides what is being counted.

Trick 6 · も turns a question into “any”

いつ is “when”. も is “also, even”. Put them together and you get at any when — which is exactly what いつも, “always”, means. Nothing has to be memorised; the word says what it is.

And this is not a one-off. The same も works on the other stems: だれも is “anyone”, どこも is “everywhere”, なにも is “anything”. You have not been taught those yet and there is a wrinkle waiting in how they behave with negatives — but when they arrive they will not be new words, they will be this rule again.

Write ちょっと with the small ょ. There is a rarer word ちっと with much the same meaning, but it is not the one in ordinary use.

ちょっと also contains the small っ, which this course has still not taught — the same character that kept どっち out of the grid in section 2. It is now the only thing left blocking, and it is one character.

Trick 7 · Store them in the order Japanese writes a date

Japanese dates run large to small — year, then month, then day — the opposite of the English habit and the same as an ISO timestamp. The six words above are in exactly that order, and the order is free storage: recite ねん · がつ · にち · ようび · じ · ぷん once as a descending scale and you have the sequence for life. Rehearsing them alphabetically, or in the order they happen to appear on a sheet, throws that away.

Two of these show older lessons still working. なんぷん is なん + ふん, and the ふ takes a maru because it lands directly after ん — which is the rule lesson 5 gave for where maru kana are allowed to live, happening in front of you. なんがつ is the tenten doing the same job on か.

A point in time, and a stretch of it

Every word above asks which — which year, which hour. None of them asks how long. The remaining four words on the sheet are how you ask that, and they are not new words at all.

Trick 8 · かん is the gap between, and it turns a point into a stretch

かん is 間 — a gap, an interval, the space between two things. Hang it on the end of a time question and the question stops pointing at a moment and starts measuring a distance:

Two units refuse to play along, and both are worth knowing as exceptions rather than discovering as mistakes:

  • Month swaps its counter instead of adding かん. なんがつ asks which month; なんかげつ nankagetsu asks how many. Different counter, not a suffix.
  • Day does not bother at all. なんにち is both “what date” and “how many days” — one word, two jobs, and context does the rest. This is exactly why your notes gloss it twice, once on each page. That was never a mistake.

Arabic makes the identical cut and makes it with two different words, which is why this is easier to hold from the Arabic side than from the English: أَيُّ سَنَةٍ is “which year”, كَمْ سَنَةً is “how many years”. Where Japanese adds かん, Arabic switches أَيُّ for كَمْ. Same distinction, two ways of drawing it.

なんしゅうかん has no dictionary entry of its own, and neither does どこの or だれと from section 3. That is the same fact each time: a dictionary does not list a phrase the language can generate. It also needs the small ゅ — lesson 7.

Trick 9 · Say both and let your mouth choose

There is nothing to memorise here, because the rule is not arbitrary. t, d and n are all made with the tongue tip against the ridge behind your teeth — and so is ん when something follows it. なんです keeps the tongue in one place; なにです makes it travel and come back. Say the wrong one aloud once and you will hear why nobody does.

The one place the ear does not help is a counter that starts with some other sound — なんかい, “how many times”. Counters take なん regardless. That is the only clause worth remembering as a rule.

The Arabic that maps onto this

Three of today's patterns have an exact partner in Arabic, which makes the translation direction in the drill much less work than it looks:

  • どの ↔ أَيّ — both attach to a following noun and neither can stand alone.
  • なん + counter ↔ كَمْ + a singular noun. Both languages take a counting word and one unit; Arabic then puts that unit in the accusative (كَمْ يَوْمًا), which is the closest thing it has to a counter.
  • なに ↔ مَاذَا — the open “what”, with no set to choose from.
  • いく ↔ كَمْ again, and the tail decides what is counted exactly as the Arabic preposition does: いくつ ↔ كَمْ عَدَدًا for things, いくら ↔ بِكَمْ for money.

8.Put one in a sentence

A question word on its own is a flashcard. To use it you need one frame, and one is genuinely enough for everything below:

〜は 〜ですか。

は marks what the sentence is about — read it wa, not ha, which is the single most useful irregularity in written Japanese. ですか you already say every day: it is the tail of おげんきですか from lesson 1. Put the question word where the answer will go and the sentence is finished.

    kore wa ikura desu ka

    Arabic

    بِكَمْ هٰذَا؟

    bi-kam hādhā

    The first sentence you will actually need in a shop, and the reason いくら is worth learning before いくつ.

  10. 10.How many hours is the break?

    Japanese

    やすみは なんじかんですか。

    yasumi wa nanjikan desu ka

    Arabic

    كَمْ سَاعَةً تَدُومُ الِاسْتِرَاحَةُ؟

    kam sāʿatan tadūmu l-istirāḥa

    Compare sentence 7, which asked the same thing with どのぐらい. That one leaves the unit open; this one demands the answer in hours. Swap なんじかん for なんじ and you are suddenly asking what time the break starts — one syllable, a different question.

  11. 11.Why? — among friends

    Japanese

    なんでですか。

    nan de desu ka

    Arabic

    لِمَ؟

    lima

    Sentence 8 with the register changed, which is the only difference between them. Say this one to a friend and どうしてですか to anyone else — and if you are writing it down, なぜですか.
