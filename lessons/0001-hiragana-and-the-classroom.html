Lesson 1 · Day 1 notes

The first 25 hiragana, and a classroom in two languages

Twenty-five characters that are really five sounds in five outfits — then sixteen sentences a teacher actually says, in Japanese and in Arabic at the same time.

≈18 minutes · stroke data from KanjiVG

Lesson 1 of the course

1.Five sounds, not twenty-five shapes

The chart is laid out as a grid, and that is the whole secret. It is not a list of twenty-five unrelated letters. It is five vowel sounds running down the columns, and a consonant bolted onto the front of each one as you move down the rows.

rowaiueo
vowelあいうえお
kかきくけこ
sさしすせそ
tたちつてと
nなにぬねの

So you are not memorising twenty-five things. You are learning five vowel sounds — a, i, u, e, o — and then four consonants that ride on top of them. Say the top row out loud once and the other four rows become predictable.

Each character is one syllable, always. Unlike Arabic or English, a hiragana character can never be a bare consonant: か is always ka, never just k. This is why Japanese is written without spaces and still readable — every character is a complete beat.

Three characters break the pattern

If the grid were perfectly regular, the s and t rows would give you si, ti, tu. They don't. し is shi, ち is chi, and つ is tsu. Those three irregular sounds are the most common early mistake — learn them as exceptions now rather than un-learning them later.

2.Write them, hear them, use them

Tap any character below. It draws itself in the correct stroke order — numbered, one stroke at a time — says itself aloud, and gives you three real words that begin with it, each one glossed in English and in Arabic. Stroke order is not decoration: it is what makes handwriting legible to a Japanese reader, and getting it wrong is visible in the finished shape.

あ い う え お か き く け こ さ し す せ そ た ち つ て と な に ぬ ね の

The interactive chart needs JavaScript. The 25 characters are listed above in row order.

Practise this way, not that way

Watch the animation once, then write the character on paper from memory before replaying it. Copying while looking feels productive and teaches you almost nothing; the effort of recalling the shape is what makes it stick. Getting it wrong and then checking beats getting it right by tracing.

3.Sixteen sentences, twice over

Sixteen phrases — the things actually said in a classroom, rather than the ones a textbook opens with. Each appears in Japanese and in Modern Standard Arabic, both with a play button.

The Japanese kana you have already learned are highlighted. You can read some of the hiragana on this page already, having started today.

    Japanese

    Your first fully readable Japanese word — all three characters are in today's chart.

  3. 3.Everyone — addressing a group.

    Japanese

    みなさん

    minasan

    Arabic

    أَيُّهَا الْجَمِيعُ

    ayyuhā l-jamīʿ

    The さん on the end is the same respectful さん added to people's names. It is what makes this "ladies and gentlemen" rather than a blunt "you lot".

  4. 4.Teacher.

    Japanese

    せんせい

    sensei

    Arabic

    اَلْمُعَلِّمُ

    al-muʿallim

    Used to address your teacher directly, the way Arabic uses يَا أُسْتَاذُ. You never add さん to it — せんせい is already respectful.

  5. 5.Excuse me. / I'm sorry.

    Japanese

    すみません

    sumimasen

    Arabic

    عَفْوًا

    ʿafwan

    Does double duty: getting attention, and apologising. Arabic splits the two — عَفْوًا to interrupt, آسِفٌ (āsif) to apologise.

  6. 6.How are you?

    Japanese

    おげんき ですか

    o-genki desu ka

    Arabic

    كَيْفَ حَالُكَ؟

    kayfa ḥāluka?

    Both languages mark who they are speaking to, but differently. Arabic changes the ending by gender — حَالُكَ to a man, حَالُكِ (ḥāluki) to a woman. Japanese changes nothing for gender; it changes the お instead, which is politeness, not grammar.

  7. 7.Yes, I'm well.

    Japanese

    はい、げんき です

    hai, genki desu

    Arabic

    نَعَمْ، أَنَا بِخَيْرٍ

    naʿam, anā bi-khayrin

    Note the お has vanished. You put it on the question because it is about the other person; you drop it in the answer because politeness is never pointed at yourself.

    Japanese

    Arabic

    Japanese

    hai, wakarimashita

    naʿam, fahimtu

    Compare this with number 8 in both languages. Section 4 is entirely about what changed.

  10. 10.Have you finished?

    Japanese

    おわりましたか

    owarimashita ka

    Arabic

    هَلِ انْتَهَيْتَ؟

    hal-i ntahayta?

    You already read the kasra on هَلِ without thinking about it. It is there because انْتَهَيْتَ opens with hamzat waṣl, which drops out and would leave two unpronounceable consonants colliding.

    Japanese

    Arabic

  12. 12.Please write.

    Japanese

    かいて ください

    kaite kudasai

    Arabic

    اُكْتُبْ مِنْ فَضْلِكَ

    uktub min faḍlika

    ください is the "please" half and never changes. Swap the word in front of it and you have a different request — exactly as مِنْ فَضْلِكَ stays put while the Arabic command changes.

  13. 13.Please wait a moment.

    Japanese

    Note the small ょ in ちょっと — written full-size, よ would make it chiyotto.

    Japanese

    こんにちは

    Arabic

    نَهَارُكَ سَعِيدٌ

    nahāruka saʿīd

4.What the two languages disagree about

Sentences 8 and 9 are the same verb twice, once as a question and once as an answer. Watching what each language changes tells you more than either sentence does alone.

Both languages ask by adding one word, and changing nothing else. Japanese hangs か on the end; Arabic puts هَلْ on the front. The words in between stay exactly as they were. English cannot do this — "you understood" has to be reshuffled into "did you understand". Two languages agree with each other and disagree with English.

 statementquestion
Japaneseわかりましたわかりましたか
Arabicفَهِمْتَهَلْ فَهِمْتَ؟

But they disagree completely about who did it. Japanese わかりました is identical whether I understood or you understood — the sentence simply does not say, and the situation supplies it. Arabic refuses to leave it open, and marks it on the very last vowel of the verb:

Arabicreadsmeans
فَهِمْتُfahimtuI understood
فَهِمْتَfahimtayou understood (to a man)
فَهِمْتِfahimtiyou understood (to a woman)

The habit to build

Same three letters, ف ه م, every time. The only thing that moves is the harakah on the final ت — and that mark is not decoration, it is the subject of the sentence. Read the last vowel of a past-tense verb first: it tells you who is speaking before the rest of the sentence does.

The same split runs through everything else on the page. انْتَهَيْتُ versus انْتَهَيْتَ is the identical trick with a different root, and Japanese おわりました again declines to say. Japanese leaves the person to context; Arabic bakes it into the word. Neither is harder — but you have to listen for different things.

Keep the hiragana stroke chart and the classroom phrasebook open while you practise — both are built to print.
