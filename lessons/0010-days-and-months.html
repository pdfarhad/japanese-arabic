Lesson 10 · こよみ — the calendar

Seven planets, twelve numbers

Japanese names its days after the planets and numbers its months. Arabic does the exact opposite — it numbers its days and borrows names for its months. Between them the two languages cover the whole calendar twice, and neither invented any of it.

≈20 minutes · every reading checked against Jisho.org · the calendar history from National Astronomical Observatory of Japan and the National Diet Library

Lesson 10 of the course

Nine lessons have been about characters. This one is the first about words you will need on a Monday — and it arrives with the first kanji in the course, because the days of the week cannot be written without them.

About the kanji on this page

Every weekday and every month here is shown in kanji as well as kana. They are for recognition only — enough to read a timetable, a ticket or a calendar. There is no stroke order, no writing practice and no general kanji system in this lesson. The kana is still the form to learn.

You get twenty of them almost free, because you already know their sounds: 日 月 火 水 木 金 土 and 一 二 三 四 五 六 七 八 九 十. Seven of those ten are also the seven days.

1.Each language numbers the half the other names

Before any vocabulary, the shape of the thing. There are two ways a language can label a repeating cycle: give each slot a name, or count. Japanese and Arabic each do one for days and the other for months — and they disagree about which is which.

The seven daysThe twelve months
Japanese Named — after the sun, the moon and five planets Numbered — month 1 to month 12, no names at all
Arabic Numbered — the first, the second … the fifth, then two named Named — borrowed, either from Latin or from Babylon
English Named — Norse gods standing in for the same planets Named — Roman

English numbers neither, which is why it is the hardest of the three to learn and why you had to memorise thirty days hath September as a child. Japanese months cost you nothing: if you can count to twelve, you know all of them by the end of section 4.

2.The seven days are the seven planets

Every weekday is one character plus 曜日 yōbi. Change the character, change the day — that is the whole system. The character names one of the seven bodies the ancient world could see moving against the stars.

Trick 1 · Learn the planet and the day comes free

Five of the seven day names begin with exactly the same reading as the planet. Not a similar one — the same one.

火星 kasei, Mars → 火曜日 kayōbi. 水星 suisei, Mercury → 水曜日 suiyōbi. And the same for 木, 金 and 土.

The other two are the Sun and the Moon, which have no 星 sei because they are not planets. That is not an exception to remember — it is astronomy.

Tap any word to hear it. The middle column is the sky; read straight down it and you have the classical planetary order, which is also the order of the Japanese week.

The Arabic column is the mirror. الْأَحَد is literally the first, الْاِثْنَيْن the second, and the count runs to الْخَمِيس, the fifth. Then it stops: الْجُمُعَة is the gathering and السَّبْت is the rest. Both languages break their own pattern at exactly the point where the week stops being arithmetic and starts being about what people do.

Trick 2 · Neither language invented its week — so the two agree more than they look

The seven-day week is Babylonian. It reached Japan as astrology and reached Arabic as a translation: Wiktionary records that the numbered Arabic day names are a phono-semantic matching of Syriac ܐܪܒܥܐ ܒܫܒܐ, “fourth of the week”, and that they replaced older native Arabic names — دُبَار for Wednesday, جُبَار for Tuesday.

So when Japanese 土曜日 (Saturn) lines up with English Saturday (Saturn) and Arabic السَّبْت (Sabbath) sits in the same slot, that is not coincidence. It is one cycle, borrowed three times.

3.Japan had the names for a thousand years before it had the week

This is the part that makes the seven names stick, because it explains why they are astrological rather than practical.

  • 806. The monk 空海 Kūkai returns from Tang China with over 460 volumes of esoteric Buddhist texts. Among them is the 宿曜経 Sukuyōkyō, an Indian astrology manual translated into Chinese by Amoghavajra around 759. It carries the seven luminaries — and with them, the names of the seven days.
  • Heian period. The court calendar, 具注暦, prints the day of the week beside each date — not to organise the working week, but to tell you whether the day is lucky. Fujiwara no Michinaga's diary 御堂関白記 records the weekday every day. Sunday was sometimes written 密 instead: a transcription of Sogdian Myr, the god Mithra, picked up somewhere along the road from Babylon.
  • Until 1876. Nobody rested on Sunday, because there was no week to rest in. Government offices closed on days containing a 1 or a 6 — the 1st, 6th, 11th, 16th, 21st and 26th — a system called 一六日 ichirokubi, fixed by decree in 1868.
  • 12 March 1876. Cabinet Order No. 27 makes Sunday a full holiday and Saturday a half-day, effective that April. The names were a thousand and seventy years old; the week itself was new.

The 1876 date and the 一六日 system it replaced are taken from the National Astronomical Observatory of Japan's calendar wiki, which cites the original decrees held by the National Diet Library. It is worth reading over the usual textbook line: the popular claim is simply “Japan adopted the week in 1876”, and the interesting fact — that the names had been in continuous use since 806 — gets lost.

4.Twelve months, twelve numbers

A month is the number plus 月 gatsu. That is the entire rule, and it has three exceptions — all three because the month names entered Japanese from Chinese as one fixed block, long before よん, なな and きゅう became the normal way to count.

Trick 3 · Four, seven and nine keep their old clothes

し, しち and く are the classical readings of 4, 7 and 9. They survive in the calendar and almost nowhere else in everyday counting, so the three odd months are not three separate facts — they are one fact about how old this vocabulary is.

Arabic goes the other way and borrows names. يَنايِر, فِبْرايِر, مارِس are Latin months in Arabic clothing, used across Egypt, Sudan, Yemen and the Gulf. Iraq and the Levant keep an older set entirely — كانُون الثانِي, شُباط, آذار — inherited from the Babylonian calendar and cousin to the Hebrew month names. Both are Modern Standard Arabic; pan-Arab news often prints both, separated by a slash. All twelve of each are on the reference sheet.

And a third Arabic option is the Japanese one: الشَّهْر السَّابِع, “the seventh month”, is ordinary speech in the Gulf and Jordan.

5.The same characters, two different words

Lesson 6's best trick was かん — the suffix that turns a point in time into a stretch of it, so なんじ asks what o'clock and なんじかん asks how many hours. The calendar is where that distinction gets sharp enough to bite, because here the two meanings share their spelling.

Trick 4 · The old readings name, the new readings count

し しち く label the months. よん なな きゅう measure them. If you hear よん anywhere near a month, you are being told a duration, not a date — and the little か in よんかげつ is the whole signal.

6.Dates are where the native numbers survived

Japanese has two number systems: the Chinese one you have been using (いち に さん) and an older native one (ひとつ ふたつ みっつ). The native one has been retreating for centuries. The first ten days of the month are its last stronghold.

Read the last two columns together and the list stops being ten arbitrary words: ふたつ → ふつか, みっつ → みっか. The か on the end is an old word for day in its own right.

The three that are not in that pattern

  • ついたち, the 1st, is not ひとつ anything. It is 月立ち tsukitachi — “the moon rising”, the day the new moon began the month in the old lunar calendar. It has outlived the calendar that made it.
  • はつか, the 20th, is the last trace of はた, an old word for twenty — the same one inside はたち, “twenty years old”. Never にじゅうにち.
  • よっか keeps going up the calendar: the 14th is じゅうよっか and the 24th is にじゅうよっか.

Everything else from the 11th on is simply the number plus にち. The full 1-to-31 table is on the reference sheet.

Watch よっか against ようか — the 4th against the 8th. This is the pair learners actually confuse, and counting beats will not save you: both are three, よ・っ・か against よ・う・か. What differs is what the middle beat is — silence held in the throat for よっか, a held o for ようか. Both of those are lesson 9's small ッ and lesson 7's long vowel, met again where getting them wrong moves an appointment by four days. Say them back to back.

7.This one, last one, next one

Three prefixes cover a week, a month and a year at once — こん this, せん previous, らい coming. Nine words for the price of three, except that the year row refuses twice.

Arabic runs the same three-way split with the same economy, and it is worth learning as a set for exactly the same reason — هٰذَا this, الْمَاضِي the past one, الْقَادِم the coming one, attached to الْأُسْبُوع, الشَّهْر and السَّنَة. Unlike Japanese, Arabic makes no exception for the year.

9.Ask for a date

No new grammar — the same 〜は 〜ですか。 frame from lesson 6, and its answer with the か dropped. What is new is that lesson 6 taught you three questions you had no way to answer: なんようび, なんがつ and なんにち. They have answers now.

〜は 〜ですか。

  1. 1.What day of the week is it today?

    Japanese

    きょうは なんようびですか。

    kyō wa nan'yōbi desu ka

    Arabic

    أَيُّ يَوْمٍ هُوَ الْيَوْمُ؟

    ayyu yawmin huwa al-yawm

    Japanese

    al-yawmu huwa al-khamīs

  3. 3.What month is your birthday?

    Japanese

    たんじょうびは なんがつですか。

    tanjōbi wa nangatsu desu ka

    Arabic

    فِي أَيِّ شَهْرٍ عِيدُ مِيلَادِكَ؟

    fī ayyi shahrin ʿīdu mīlādika

  4. 4.When is the holiday?

    Japanese

    やすみは いつですか。

    yasumi wa itsu desu ka

    Arabic

    مَتَى الْعُطْلَةُ؟

    matā al-ʿuṭla

  5. 5.It is next Friday.

    Japanese

    らいしゅうの きんようびです。

    raishū no kin'yōbi desu

    Arabic

    يَوْمُ الْجُمُعَةِ الْقَادِمِ.

    yawmu al-jumuʿati al-qādim

Sentence 5 uses の to join one noun to another: らいしゅうの きんようび is “next week's Friday”, which is how Japanese says “next Friday”.
