Lesson 8 · カタカナ — the second script

Twenty-five new shapes, no new sounds

Today's chart is day one's chart with every character swapped out. The same five rows, the same five vowels down the columns, the same three irregulars — and not one sound you have not been saying since the first lesson.

Lesson 8 of the course

Japanese runs on two syllabaries, not one. They spell exactly the same sounds; what differs is which words get written in which. Everything so far has been hiragana. This is the other one, and because the sound system is already learned, the entire job today is recognising twenty-five shapes.

Trick 1 · Do not learn twenty-five characters. Learn twenty-five spellings of things you can already say

Every other lesson so far added sounds: new consonants, voiced rows, a contracted beat. This one adds none. When you meet カ the answer is not "work out what this says" but "which character I already know is this the other spelling of?" That is a much cheaper question, and it is the only question this chart asks.

Trick 2 · The three irregulars are the same three

If the grid were regular the s and t rows would give si, ti, tu. They don't — and they don't in exactly the same three places as before. シ is shi, チ is chi, ツ is tsu. There is no second list of exceptions to learn; it is the same list, worn by different characters.

2.Why the shapes look the way they look

Both syllabaries were made in the Heian period out of the same raw material: Chinese characters that had been pressed into service to spell Japanese sounds, called man'yōgana. What separates them is not which characters they started from but what was done to them.

That one difference explains the whole page. な is the kanji 奈 written fast, so it curls and loops through four strokes. ナ is the top of 奈 taken cleanly and left straight, so it is two. Same ancestor, opposite treatment.

Trick 3 · Katakana are not fewer strokes — they are straighter strokes

It is tempting to assume the angular script is the quicker one. It is not. These twenty-five katakana take 62 strokes between them; the twenty-five hiragana take 57. Cursive writing joins strokes together, so つ and て are one stroke each where ツ and テ are three. What katakana buy you is that every stroke is a straight line or a simple hook — easier to get right, not fewer to make.

Fifteen are siblings. Eight are strangers. Two are arguments.

Because the two scripts were cut from the same stock of man'yōgana, some pairs really do descend from one character — and where they do, the family resemblance is inherited and can be trusted. But not all of them do. Fifteen of these twenty-five pairs share an ancestor; eight came from completely different kanji and have no reason to look alike at all; and for two of them the sources do not agree.

The remaining two are worth knowing about, because they are where the scholarship itself stops agreeing. ツ has no settled ancestor at all: the Nihon Kokugo Daijiten lists 川, 州, 津 and 闘, and つ is unsettled in the same way. ニ is unsettled differently: sources split evenly between 仁 and 二, and since に definitely comes from 仁, whether this pair are siblings is exactly what is in dispute. The shape comes out the same either way — 仁 is 亻 plus 二 — so the writing is settled even though the descent is not.

Trick 4 · Check whether a pair is related before you go looking for a likeness

This is the difference between a structural hook and a made-up one. コ really is こ with its right-hand ends joined, because both are 己 — lean on that. サ and さ also look a bit alike, and it means nothing: 散 and 左 are unrelated characters and the resemblance is an accident. Building a memory on the second kind is how you end up confidently writing the wrong letter. Tap any character in the next section and its card says which kind it is.

Practise this way, not that way

Watch the animation once, then write the character on paper from memory before replaying it. Copying while looking feels productive and teaches you almost nothing; the effort of recalling the shape is what makes it stick. With katakana there is a second trap: because the shapes are simple, a wrong one still looks plausible. Recall it, then check — do not trace.

4.The pairs that get misread

Simple shapes made of straight strokes are quick to write and easy to confuse. Which pairs actually cause trouble is not a matter of opinion: a 2019 writing study tested all 45 katakana on 430 Japanese children in school years 1–3 and counted the errors.4 Its finding is blunt — ツ and シ lead the list, and around ten in every 134 children were still getting them wrong in year three.

Two more that get listed in beginners' material and are worth a smaller share of your attention: ク against タ (one extra slash, three strokes against two), and ク against ワ and コ against ユ, both of which wait for the second half of the chart. ク/タ is worth knowing but is not what trips people: neither character appears anywhere in that study's error data.

Trick 5 · For シ and ツ, read the stroke direction

Both are two short marks and one long sweep, and printed side by side they are nearly the same picture. What separates them is the direction the pen travels, which the finished shape only hints at:

  • シ — shi: the two marks come in from the left, roughly horizontal, and the long stroke is pulled upward from the bottom left. Its hiragana し also ends by sweeping up.
  • ツ — tsu: the two marks come down from the top, roughly vertical, and the long stroke is pulled downward to the left. Its hiragana つ also runs left along the top.

Read off a finished letter, there are two independent giveaways and they point the same way. Where the strokes start: all three of シ's begin in a column down the left-hand side, while all three of ツ's begin in a row along the top. How the two short marks are angled: shallow, close to horizontal, on シ; steeper, close to upright, on ツ. And the same test, applied to one mark instead of two, is what will later separate ソ from ン — シ and ン are one family, ツ and ソ the other.

5.Read them cold

Everything above this line you could do while looking at the chart. This is the part that finds out whether the shapes are actually in your head: a character appears, you name its sound, four options, by tapping or by pressing 1–4. There is deliberately no play button on the question — hearing it is the answer. Get it right and the drill moves straight on. Get it wrong and it stops, tells you the hiragana that shares the sound, how the strokes go, and every word from the cards above with that character inside it.

The three wrong answers are not random, and after section 4 you can guess where they come from. They are drawn first from the pairs this lesson has flagged — シ against ツ, ス against ヌ, ク against ケ — then from the same row, and only then from anywhere. A multiple-choice question whose wrong answers are obviously wrong measures nothing.

One round is all twenty-five, shuffled. Miss some and it offers you those again on their own. Nothing is saved anywhere — this is a mirror, not a record.

6.What katakana is actually for

Hiragana and katakana spell the same sounds, so which one a word is written in is never about pronunciation — it is a label on the word. Seeing katakana tells you something about a word before you have finished reading it.

  1. Loanwords — foreign words taken into Japanese (gairaigo), which is by far the commonest use. Also foreign place names and foreign personal names: the United States is アメリカ.
  2. Onomatopoeia — words that are sounds.
  3. Technical and scientific terms, including the scientific names of animal and plant species and of minerals. This is why ネコ, キツネ and スイカ appear on the cards above in katakana even though they are ordinary Japanese words, not borrowings — but see the caution below, because this one is routinely overstated.
  4. Company names, which is how a firm is told apart from the surname it shares.
  5. Signs and advertising.
  6. Emphasis — doing the job italics do in English.

Where point 3 gets overstated — and it usually does

"Animals and plants are written in katakana" is most of the way to true and is worth being careful with. Japan's Agency for Cultural Affairs sets the actual rule for official writing:5 as an ordinary word, an animal or plant name is written in kanji if the character is on the standard list and in kana if it is not — so 犬 stays 犬. Katakana is the convention for the scientific name. The Ichthyological Society of Japan states it outright for fish: species names below the rank of order are to be written in katakana.6

So イヌ is closer to "Canis familiaris" than to "dog". Reading it as a plain synonym is the mistake.

And the reason is not that biology likes katakana. Before the war, ordinary Japanese was written in kanji and katakana, and species names were set in hiragana to stand out from it. After the post-war script reforms flipped the everyday mixture to kanji and hiragana, species names flipped too — to katakana. It is a contrast device: the name is written in whichever script the surrounding text is not.

Trick 6 · Sound it out in English before you look it up

Because most katakana words are borrowed, reading one often means recognising a word you already have. テレビ is terebi is television. ネクタイ is nekutai is necktie. スーパー is sūpā is supermarket. Say the syllables out loud and let your ear finish the word — it succeeds far more often than it has any right to, and it is the single fastest reading skill in Japanese.

It is not a guarantee. ケース is case, but the sense narrows to a container. And point 3 above means plenty of katakana words are not borrowed at all.

One word this course was always going to reach

コーヒー — kōhī — reached Japanese from Dutch koffie, and Dutch got it, through a chain of European languages, from Arabic قَهْوَة (qahwa).2 The Japanese card above and the Arabic gloss beside it are, historically, the same word arriving from two directions. Most loanwords on this page record only their most recent donor — English or Dutch — but this one is traced the whole way.

The one thing today's sheet does not give you is the long-vowel mark ー, which katakana uses constantly to hold a vowel for a second beat: it is the difference between コヒ and コーヒー. It appears in the words on the cards above because they would be misspelled without it, but it is not part of the twenty-five, and neither are the small kana or the marks. Those arrive with the rest of the chart.
