1.Two more rows of the same grid

The first eight words are not new shapes. They are the こ, そ and あ columns of two more rows of the こそあど grid — the one lesson 6 drew when it taught the ど column, and lesson 12 filled in two rows of.

The grid needs JavaScript. The same sets are laid out on the question-word reference sheet.

Four rows of the grid are now yours: れ and の from lesson 12, こ and ちら from today. Learn a row and you get four words; learn the three prefixes once and they never change again.

Trick 1 · The ちら row is the polite row of the whole grid

The lecture sheet writes “polite equivalent of ここ” beside each of the four ちら words and never says why. The why is that they ask a direction where the short ones ask a place, and gesturing at a direction is vaguer — therefore gentler — than pointing at a spot.

That is chapter 11's rule turning up for the third time: the polite form is longer, and it is less direct. だれ → どなた, いくつ → おいくつ, and now どこ → どちら.

Trick 2 · Arabic builds its place words from the same two pieces as yesterday

Lesson 12 found that Arabic marks its middle demonstrative with كاف الخطاب, the second-person you, and its far one by adding لام البعد on top: ذَا → ذَاكَ → ذٰلِكَ. The place words do it again, on a different stem:

  • هُنَا — here. Bare.
  • هُنَاكَ — there. Plus the you.
  • هُنَالِكَ — over there. Plus the lām and the you, and en.wiktionary gives that etymology outright: ـكَ + لِ + هُنَا.

So both languages derive place from pointing by a regular operation — Japanese swaps the ending (これ → ここ), Arabic stacks two suffixes. Learn the operation once and you get a paradigm in each language.

3.Japanese counts in fours

This is the part of the chapter that has to be understood rather than memorised. English and Arabic put a separator every three digits. Japanese groups every four.

Put side by side, the question is simply which numbers does each language have a single word for:

NumberEnglishArabicJapanese
100hundredمِئَةひゃく 百
1,000thousandأَلْفせん 千
10,000ten thousandعَشَرَةُ آلَافٍまん 万
1,000,000millionمِلْيُونひゃくまん 百万
100,000,000hundred millionمِئَةُ مِلْيُونٍおく 億

Italic means the language has no word of its own and builds one out of smaller pieces. Read down the columns: English and Arabic go up in threes and have to spell out 10,000; Japanese goes up in fours and has to spell out 1,000,000. Neither is stranger than the other — but you have to switch.

Trick 3 · まん is a column, not a word — so it is never said alone

Ten thousand is いちまん, never まん. From 万 upward a number takes its 一 when nothing else precedes it, where 百 and 千 stand bare — ひゃく and せん are fine on their own.

The reliable test: if you can say it in English without the word one, Japanese probably needs the 一. And Arabic agrees with English here — أَلْف stands alone too. This is the one place the two languages you have both diverge from the one you are learning.

4.The sounds that collide

Numbers meeting counters change shape. Nobody chose these; they are what happens when the consonants meet. Only the marked cells differ from the predictable form.

Two things are worth taking from that table rather than memorising it whole. Six and eight double the consonant — ろっ, はっ — which is the small っ lesson 7 taught, doing a job. And three voices what follows it: ひゃく → びゃく, せん → ぜん, かい → がい. That is the tenten from lesson 5, appearing as a sound rule rather than a spelling one.

Trick 4 · The question word tells you the rule

The lecture sheet lists なんがい — what floor — as its own row, voiced. It does not list さんがい anywhere. But なんがい is proof enough: if the question word voices, the counter voices, and 3階 is さんがい.

Same trick reads the brackets on the sheet's own 〜かい（〜がい） row. That parenthesis is not an alternative pronunciation — it is the entire sound change, compressed into one cell.

7.Buying something

Four sentences carry the whole transaction. The frame is the one you have — 〜は 〜です with lesson 6's か — plus today's numbers.

これは いくらですか。

JapaneseEnglish
これは いくらですか。How much is this?
さんぜんえんです。It's 3,000 yen.
くつうりばは なんがいですか。What floor is the shoe department on?
ごかいで ございます。It's on the fifth floor.

Trick 5 · でございます comes at you, not from you

The last line is the shop assistant's, and it is です one storey higher — the sheet glosses it only as “polite equivalent of です”. Staff say it to customers; the customer does not say it back.

Chapter 11 established that Japanese politeness is directional — it points away from the speaker. せんせい about others and きょうし about yourself was the first case, おくに on this page is the second, and this is the third. You will hear でございます far more often than you will ever need to say it.

Three set phrases finish the exchange, in the order they are spoken.

    Japanese

    Arabic

  2. 2.Please show me that one.

    Japanese

    sore o misete kudasai

    Arabic

    أَرِنِي ذَاكَ، مِنْ فَضْلِكَ.

    arinī dhāka, min faḍlika

  3. 3.Right — I'll take it.

    Japanese

    じゃ、それを ください。

    ja, sore o kudasai

    Arabic

    إِذَنْ، أَعْطِنِي ذَاكَ مِنْ فَضْلِكَ.

    idhan, aʿṭinī dhāka min faḍlika

Learn these two as shapes, not as grammar

Both requests contain something this course has not taught and is not teaching today. The を marks what the verb is done to, and みせて is a conjugated form of みせる. Take the whole phrase as one block — swap only the word in front of を — exactly as chapter 11 handled 〜から きました and chapter 12 handled これから おせわに なります.

ください on its own asks for a thing; みせて ください asks for an action. That difference is the entire reason one of them carries the extra word.
