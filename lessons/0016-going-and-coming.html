About the verbs on this page

Three verbs arrive today, each with a Roman numeral beside it, and the standing rule from chapter 14 has not moved: learn each word with its ます already attached, as one fixed shape. The numerals are for building a verb's other forms, and this course does not build them yet.

One thing has changed since chapter 14: this time all three markers are right — including the one on かえります, which is the classic place to get it wrong. See section 1.

1.Three verbs, two directions

English chooses come or go by all sorts of habits. Japanese chooses by one test: which way the movement points. Away from the speaker is いきます; toward the speaker is きます. And かえります is not simply return — it is return to your own base: home, your desk, your country.

JapaneseKanjiEnglishArabic
いきます行きますgo — away from the speakerيَذْهَبُ
きます来ますcome — toward the speakerيَأْتِي
かえります帰りますgo home, return to baseيَعُودُ إِلَى الْبَيْتِ

Trick 1 · The ます form hides what the kanji keeps

きます is 来ます, comes — group III, the group with exactly two members. But きます is also 着ます, wears, a perfectly ordinary group II verb. Same sound, same polite ending, different word, different machinery underneath. The same happens to いきます, which is 行きます goes and also 生きます lives.

The ます form erases the difference; the kanji carries it. Six chapters running, the dictionary check has made the same point — a short kana string identifies almost nothing — and verbs turn out to be no exception.

Trick 2 · かえります is the trap the sheet did not fall into

Chapter 14's sheet marked おきます with the wrong group. This sheet marks かえります I, and that is the hard call made correctly: 帰る ends in the -eru sound that group II verbs end in, and is group I anyway. It is the standard example of the trap in every grammar of Japanese. A sheet that gets this one right has been checked against something.

2.Nine ways to go

How you go is marked with で — the by-what-means particle you have had since なんで in lesson 6, finally given a fleet to answer with.

バスで いきます。

JapaneseReads asEnglish
バスで いきます。bus-by / goI go by bus.
しんかんせんで きます。Shinkansen-by / comeI come by Shinkansen.
あるいて かえります。on-foot / go homeI walk home.

The third row is the odd one out on purpose: あるいて — on foot — takes no で at all, because the manner is already baked into the word. It is a fixed shape of the verb walk, learned whole the way みせて was in chapter 13. Arabic happens to build on foot the same way — مَشْيًا is a frozen form doing adverb duty — so both languages answer how? here with a shape, not a phrase.

Trick 3 · One character decides whether you pedal

The course now has three 車 words, and the middle character does all the work: 自動車 is the self-moving vehicle — a car; 自転車 is the self-turning vehicle — a bicycle; 電車 is the electric vehicle — a train.

And 行, the character of 行きます, is hiding twice more on this sheet with its other reading, こう: 飛行機 fly-go-machine, and 急行 hurry-go. One character, two readings, three words on one page.

Trick 4 · とっきゅう is two words, squeezed by a rule you know

特急 is 特別 special + 急行 rapid, clipped to a character each. And the small っ in the middle is not decoration — it is とく's final く collapsing before a voiceless sound, exactly the gemination the counter sheet taught for 1, 6, 8 and 10: とく + きゅう → とっきゅう. A sound rule you learned for numbers, running inside ordinary vocabulary.

The Arabic on the cards makes the same cut: rapid is قِطَارٌ سَرِيع, and express adds خَاصّ — special — which is precisely the 特.

The platform is a counter: 〜ばんせん, built from 何番's 番 number and 新幹線's 線 line. It is the course's ninth, and the counter sheet's first-sound rule scores its cheapest win yet: ば is voiced, so no number ever changes it — いちばんせん, さんばんせん, なんばんせん, straight through with nothing to memorise.

Two cells on the sheet to correct

駅 has slid up a row. The sheet prints it beside スーパー, whose kanji cell should be empty — a supermarket is a clipped loanword with no kanji — and leaves えき's own cell blank one row down. 駅 is station; put it back on えき's row. This is the neighbouring-cell slip again, the same class as day 11's row that filed America under the U.K.

甲子園 is not in Osaka. The sheet says "a town in Osaka"; it is in Nishinomiya, Hyōgo Prefecture, between Osaka and Kobe — near Osaka, not in it. Both JMdict entries place it in Nishinomiya, and so does the famous baseball stadium's own address.

4.The calendar, revisited in kanji

Twenty-four rows of this sheet are words you already own — and the sheet still earns its space, because lesson 10 taught these words as sounds and the kanji column now shows they were a system all along. Three prefixes, three units, nine words:

先 last今 this来 next
週 week先週 せんしゅう今週 こんしゅう来週 らいしゅう
月 month先月 せんげつ今月 こんげつ来月 らいげつ
年 year去年 きょねん今年 ことし来年 らいねん

The bottom row breaks the grid twice, and both breaks are old words refusing to be regularised: last year is 去年 — the departed year — not 先年; and 今年 keeps the native reading ことし where the grid predicts こんねん. The same 今 read three ways — こん here, きょ in 今日, こ in 今年 — is chapter 14's いま character showing its whole range.

The sheet then re-lists the day-of-month readings — ついたち through とおか, plus はつか — all of which live on lesson 10's cards and the counter sheet. Only two dates on the page have never had a card, and they are the two lesson 10's よっか card promised: じゅうよっか, the 14th, and にじゅうよっか, the 24th — the native よっ- surviving up a calendar that has gone regular everywhere around it. Both get cards below. なんがつ, なんにち and いつ are lesson 6's, and たんじょうび is lesson 10's — revision all.

Trick 5 · The sheets keep answering each other

This is the third time a chapter has quietly completed an exchange an earlier one opened: chapter 12's こちらこそよろしく answered chapter 11's どうぞよろしく, and now どういたしまして answers chapter 12's ありがとう. Greetings in this course arrive as halves of a dialogue, one half per chapter — which is also how you will meet them in real rooms: someone else says the first line.
