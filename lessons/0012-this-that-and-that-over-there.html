1.Two rows, and the only question that matters

The six new pointing words are two rows of three. Every one of them is a distance prefix — こ, そ, あ — plus an ending, and the ending is what decides which row you need.

The grid needs JavaScript. The same sets are laid out on the question-word reference sheet.

The ど column is lesson 6's, and it has been sitting there for six lessons waiting for the other three. Read across and you learn a row; read down and you learn a distance.

Trick 1 · れ stands alone, の needs a noun

これ is the noun — it is the whole subject of the sentence and nothing may follow it. この is not a word you can finish a thought with: it must have a noun behind it, and without one the sentence is unfinished in the way English “this…” is when you trail off.

So the test is not how far away. It is am I about to say what the thing is called? If yes, use the の row. If you are only pointing, use れ.

Trick 2 · この is こ + の, and it is the same の you already know

The row ending is the linking particle from chapter 11 — the one in わたしの. So この ほん is built exactly like にほんごの ほん: something, then の, then the noun it belongs to. Nothing new is being learned here; a particle you have is being attached to a prefix you have.

2.It is territory, not distance

The middle word is the one every course gets asked about, because “that” in English covers both of the last two. Japanese is not measuring metres. It is asking whose side of the conversation the thing is on.

Trick 3 · Arabic marks the middle one with the word you

Traditional Arabic grammar sorts اسم الإشارة into three ranks, not two: القريبة near, المتوسطة middle, البعيدة far. And the middle rank is not defined by distance at all — it is defined as the forms carrying كاف الخطاب, the kāf of address, which is the second-person you stuck on the end. ذَا plus a you gives ذَاكَ. The far rank then adds لام البعد, the lām of distance, on top: ذٰلِكَ.

So both languages encode near you with a dedicated piece, and Arabic's piece is literally the word you. When you reach for それ, you are doing what ذَاكَ already does.

Worth knowing that the sources disagree, because this is exactly the kind of claim that gets repeated without checking. English Wikipedia's Arabic grammar article calls the system binary and treats ذاك and ذلك as variants of one far form; its Demonstrative article, and the Arabic-language article on اسم إشارة, both give three ranks. What none of them disputes is the part that matters here — the ك is كاف الخطاب, an addressee marker. The three-way classification is the traditional grammarians'; the morphology is not in question.

Trick 4 · Arabic uses one word for both rows, and moves the work onto the noun

Where Japanese has two shapes, هٰذَا does both jobs — and what tells them apart is whether the noun is definite:

  • هٰذَا كِتَابٌ — this is a book. Indefinite noun, no article, so the sentence is a statement.
  • هٰذَا الْكِتَابُ — this book. The الـ makes it definite, and the phrase becomes one thing you can then say something about.

Japanese puts the distinction in the demonstrative (これ / この); Arabic puts it on the noun. Same distinction, opposite end of the phrase — which is why the word cards for この, その and あの carry the الـ.

One character on the lecture sheet is wrong

めいし is printed with the kanji 名詞, which means noun — the part of speech. A business card is 名刺: same reading, and the second character is 刺, not 詞. Both are ordinary words, so nothing about the page looks odd; the English and Bangla columns have it right. Learn 名刺.

6.One question, two answers

No new grammar is needed to use any of this. The frame is chapter 6's question and chapter 11's です, with today's なん dropped into the middle.

これは なんですか。

JapaneseEnglish
これは なんですか。What is this?
それは じしょです。That is a dictionary.
あれは わたしの かばんです。That over there is my bag.
これは にほんごの ほんです。This is a Japanese book.

The last two lines are the same shape. わたしの かばん and にほんごの ほん both link two nouns with の, and so does この ほん from section 1. One particle, three jobs that are really one job.

Asked a yes-or-no version of that question, there are two replies and you will use almost nothing else:

JapaneseEnglish
それは じしょですか。Is that a dictionary?
はい、そうです。Yes, that's right.
いいえ、ちがいます。No, that's wrong.

そう is the そ of this chapter's own set — literally that way, your way. Answering そうです is agreeing with the shape the other person put the question in, which is why it only answers a question ending in a noun plus ですか. ちがいます means it differs: Japanese corrects the statement rather than the speaker, and that is why it lands more softly than a flat “no”.

Trick 5 · The shorter the kana, the less it tells you

Three of today's words are traps in kana alone, and the dictionary makes the point better than any argument could:

  • ご — one mora. The first four entries are 五 five, 後 after, 碁 the board game, and only then 語.
  • そう — the sense this chapter wants, 然う, is fifth, behind 僧 a monk and three others.
  • なん — 難 difficulty comes before 何 what.

This is the honest answer to why the lecture sheet carries a 漢字 column at all, and it is chapter 11's かんこく catch sharpened: kana records the sound, and a short sound belongs to a great many words.

7.Being given something

Five set sentences, in the order they are actually said. Together they are the whole of a small gift changing hands — which in Japan is a scripted exchange, and the script is short enough to learn in one sitting.

Phrase 5 is the useful one, and it only makes sense next to yesterday's page. どうぞ よろしく おねがいします was chapter 11's closing line; こちら こそ よろしく is what the other person says back. こちら is こそあど again — the こ member of the same ちら set as どちら, so this side means me. Two lecture sheets a day apart turn out to be two halves of one conversation.
