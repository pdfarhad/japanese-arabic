Completing the hiragana: the last twenty-one

Twenty-one characters finish the chart. Most of them are the regular pattern you already know, running on for another five rows — and then four of them refuse to behave, which is why the bottom of the chart looks broken.

1.What is left of the grid

Day 1 gave you the vowels and four consonants riding on them: k, s, t, n. Today adds the remaining four — h, m, y, r, w — and one lone character that belongs to no row at all. Twenty-five plus twenty-one is forty-six, and forty-six is the whole of hiragana.

Two of these rows are almost free. The m row and the r row are perfectly regular: five characters each, consonant plus vowel, nothing to memorise beyond the shapes. The h row is regular in four places out of five. Compared with day 1 — where three characters out of twenty-five broke the pattern — the arithmetic today is friendlier than it looks.

The r is not the r you are expecting

Japanese r is a single tap of the tongue against the ridge behind the teeth — somewhere between an English r, an l, and the d in "ladder". It is much closer to the Arabic ر in رَجُلٌ than to anything in English, except that it is tapped once and never rolled. If you already say ر cleanly, you are most of the way there.

Three shapes are worth extra minutes

ふ is four separate marks, not one flowing squiggle, and it is the character beginners most often draw as an unrecognisable blob. を and ん are rare enough on the page that they get the least practice and stay the shakiest longest. Give those three twice the repetitions of anything else in the chart.

3.Four things that don't behave

Everything else in today's chart is consonant plus vowel, exactly as advertised. These four are the exceptions, and each one is a different kind of exception.

ふ is neither hu nor fu. The sound is made with both lips brought close together and air pushed between them — as if blowing out a candle. English f and Arabic ف both put the top teeth on the bottom lip; Japanese never does. Romaji writes it fu because that is the nearer of two wrong answers. Take the teeth out of your ف and you have it.

を is a character with exactly one job. It is pronounced o — identical to お, no difference at all. It never appears inside a word. Its only use is to sit immediately after a noun and mark that noun as the thing the verb is being done to. And this is a place where the two languages are doing the same work with different tools:

 "read a book"what marks the object
Japaneseほんをよむを — a separate character, parked after the noun
Arabicيَقْرَأُ كِتَابًاthe accusative ‑an ending, written on the last letter of the noun itself

Japanese hangs a whole character off the back of the word; Arabic changes the word's final vowel and leaves the letters alone. Same grammatical idea — mark the object — solved from opposite directions.

ん is the only kana that is not a syllable. Every other character in the chart is a consonant and a vowel together. ん is a consonant on its own. But — and this is the part worth slowing down for — it still takes a full beat. Japanese counts in mora, and ん is one mora, the same length as か or ま.

Sukūn is not ん

A consonant carrying sukūn in Arabic — the نْ in مِنْ — closes the syllable it is attached to and takes no time of its own. ん looks like the same thing on paper and is not: it is its own beat. にほん is ni-ho-n, three even beats, not ni-hon in two. Arabic reading habits will make you rush it, and rushing it is the single most audible beginner accent in Japanese.

Two rows have real holes in them. The y row is missing yi and ye; the w row is missing three of its five. These are not omissions in the chart. The syllables yi and ye never existed in Japanese, and the characters ゐ (wi) and ゑ (we) were abolished in the 1946 spelling reform — you will meet them only in pre-war text. There are forty-six characters rather than fifty because the language genuinely has forty-six sounds here, not because five were left out.

4.Confusion pairs, now that both halves are taught

Yesterday's reference sheet listed some of these with a note that the partner had not been taught yet. Both halves are now yours, so the comparisons are worth making properly. Every one of these is a pair that gets mixed up in the first month, and none of them is fixed by staring — write both, side by side, and the difference lands.

5.What you can read now

Yesterday's classroom phrases had gaps in them — は, ま, り, わ and ん were not yet yours, so several sentences were half-lit. They are yours now. Everything highlighted below is a character you can read.

JapanesereadsEnglish
はい、わかりましたhai, wakarimashitaYes, I understood.
こんにちはkonnichiwaHello.
ほんをよみますhon o yomimasuI read a book.
やまとうみyama to umimountain and sea
わたしのなまえwatashi no namaemy name
ひらがなhiraganahiragana

That is most of the hiragana in the table above, which is as complete as it gets from this chart alone. The one character left dark is が — か wearing two small marks in the top right. Those marks are dakuten, and they turn a whole row of sounds voiced: k becomes g, s becomes z, t becomes d, h becomes b. No new shapes to learn — the same forty-six characters with a diacritic, exactly as ب and ت and ث are one shape with different dots. That is a later day's work, and it is a short one.
