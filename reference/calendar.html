The calendar

The seven days and the planets they are named after, all twelve months in Japanese and in both Arabic systems, every date from the 1st to the 31st, and the twelve old Japanese month names — in Japanese, English and Modern Standard Arabic. Made to print.

Taught in lesson 10 · readings from Jisho.org (JMdict) · calendar history from the National Astronomical Observatory of Japan and the National Diet Library

1.The shape of it, in two lines

  • Japanese names its days and numbers its months. A weekday is one element character plus 曜日; a month is a number plus 月.
  • Arabic does the reverse. Five days are simply counted, two are named for worship and rest; the months are borrowed names.

2.The seven days

Tap any word to hear it. The middle column is the classical planetary order, which is also the order of the Japanese week. Five day names open with exactly the reading of their planet — 火星 kasei gives 火曜日 kayōbi — and the two that do not are the Sun and the Moon, which are not planets.

The names reached Japan in 806, carried back from Tang China by Kūkai in the 宿曜経 along with over 460 other volumes, and were used for a thousand years to judge whether a day was lucky. Japan did not rest on a seven-day cycle until Cabinet Order No. 27 of 12 March 1876 made Sunday a holiday; before that, offices closed on days containing a 1 or a 6.

3.The twelve months

Number plus 月 gatsu. Three keep the classical readings of their number and are marked below. Both Arabic columns are Modern Standard: the first is used in Egypt, Sudan, Yemen and the Gulf, the second in Iraq and the Levant, and pan-Arab media often print both.

The Levantine names are Babylonian by descent and cousin to the Hebrew month names — نَيْسان is Nisan, تَمُّوز is Tammuz, أَيْلُول is Elul. Note the two pairs that count themselves: تِشْرِين الأَوَّل and تِشْرِين الثانِي are first and second Tishrin, كانُون الأَوَّل and كانُون الثانِي first and second Kanun — so December and January are the same month named twice, which is why the year break falls inside a pair. In the Gulf and Jordan you will also simply hear الشَّهْر السَّابِع, “the seventh month”, which is the Japanese system exactly.

7.The twelve old month names

Before 1873 Japan ran a lunisolar calendar whose months had names rather than numbers — the 和風月名. They are no longer how anyone tells you a date, but they are everywhere in poetry, brand names and personal names, and two are still ordinary words: やよい and しわす.

Etymologies from the National Diet Library's 和風月名, which is explicit that they are 諸説あり — several accounts exist and none is settled. Two things are worth knowing about them. They sit one to two months later than the modern month of the same number, which is why the eighth month is about falling leaves; and the old almanacs did not actually print them — Edo-period calendars wrote 正月・二月・三月, plain numbers, exactly as Japanese does today.
